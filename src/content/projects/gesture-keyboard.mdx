## The problem

Gesture-driven text entry has been demonstrated many times and adopted almost never. Surveying the prior work, the same three failures recur:

**The vocabulary is too small.** Systems that recognise five to seven gestures cannot express a keyboard. Typing becomes a mode-switching puzzle.

**Lighting breaks everything.** Approaches built on raw pixels — HSV thresholding, CNNs over cropped hand images — degrade sharply when the light changes, and most papers only report one lighting condition.

**Real time is claimed, not measured.** A two-second delay per keystroke, or an unreported frame rate, disqualifies a system for actual use no matter how good the accuracy is.

And nearly all of it is English-only. Bangla — roughly 240 million speakers — has almost no touchless text-entry support.

## The core idea: don't classify the gesture

The pipeline is deliberately **non-probabilistic** at the recognition stage.

MediaPipe already solves the hard perception problem: it returns 21 normalised 3D hand landmarks per frame. Once you have those coordinates, a gesture is a *geometric configuration* — a set of joint angles and inter-landmark distances — not an image to be classified.

So instead of training a CNN on hand crops, the system encodes the landmark geometry directly and decodes the gesture with explicit rules.

Three things fall out of that choice:

- **Latency drops to under 0.03 ms per gesture.** There is no forward pass. It is arithmetic on 21 points.
- **Lighting robustness is inherited**, not learned. MediaPipe handles illumination; downstream geometry is invariant to it. The paper validates this across **seven lighting conditions**.
- **Adding a gesture means writing a constraint**, not collecting and labelling a dataset.

The tradeoff is real and worth stating: this works for *static* gestures. Dynamic, trajectory-based gestures would need the temporal model back.

## Where learning is actually needed

Recognition is geometry. Prediction is where a model earns its place.

Two-layer LSTMs handle **next-word prediction in English and Bangla**, which is what makes gesture typing fast enough to tolerate — you select a predicted word rather than spelling it out one gesture at a time.

Results: **above 97% accuracy with perplexity below 1.15**, giving character error rates of **3.43% for English and 8.34% for Bangla**.

The Bangla gap is the honest, interesting part. It is a morphologically richer script with a larger effective vocabulary and far less training data available. The paper reports the gap rather than averaging the two languages into one flattering number.

## The system around it

A unified interface maps recognised gestures to both key presses and pointer actions, so keyboard and mouse are one modality rather than two modes. A lightweight monitor logs frame rate, latency and resource usage continuously, which is what allowed the cross-platform statistical comparison — the system was benchmarked on multiple hardware configurations, not one developer laptop.

Everything runs sequentially in real time: **up to 37.7 FPS** end to end.
